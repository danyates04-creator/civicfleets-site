What Is Fleet Management? A Guide for City and Public Works Fleets

By Dan Yates, Founder & Fleet Consultant, Civic Fleet Services · October 17, 2026

Short answer: Fleet management for a city or public works department is how the fleet team buys, maintains, fuels, tracks and replaces public vehicles and equipment, from patrol cars and plows to mowers. The goal is to keep services running, keep preventive maintenance on schedule, and be able to show every dollar to council and the public.

I've spent more than 20 years in fleet maintenance, including time in the Fleet Manager's chair for public fleets. Most of what you'll find online about fleet management is written for delivery companies and trucking outfits. That's fine if you run routes for profit. It doesn't help much when your fleet is a mix of police units, dump trucks, a loader, and forty pieces of small equipment, and your boss is a city council.

This guide is written for the city side.

Fleet management, defined

Fleet management is the work of keeping a group of vehicles and equipment safe, available and paid for over their whole life. That covers buying them, maintaining them, fueling them, tracking what they cost, and getting rid of them at the right time.

In a commercial fleet, the measuring stick is usually profit. Fewer miles and lower cost per stop.

In a public fleet, the measuring stick is service. Can the street crew get out after a storm? Is the patrol car ready at shift change? Did the mower get its service before the season? Nobody bills a resident per pothole, so the fleet's job is to keep departments working and to spend public money in a way you can defend later.

A few other things set government fleets apart:

  • You buy through public procurement rules, which usually means quotes, bids or a cooperative contract.
  • Your records may be open to public records requests, and auditors can review them.
  • Your budget runs on a fiscal year, and big purchases often need council approval.
  • You probably run your own shop, with your own technicians and parts room.

What a public-sector fleet manager does

The job title sounds like it's mostly desk work. It isn't. A good public fleet manager spends a lot of time in the shop, on the phone with departments, and in budget meetings.

Preventive maintenance by miles, hours and calendar

Preventive maintenance (PM) is the core of the job. If PM slips, everything else gets harder: more breakdowns, more overtime, more units sitting on the line waiting for parts.

The catch in a city fleet is that one schedule doesn't fit everything. A patrol car racks up miles. A backhoe racks up engine hours and may barely move on the odometer. A generator or a seasonal plow might sit for months, so it needs service on a calendar basis whether it ran or not.

So you track PM three ways: by miles, by hours, and by days. Whichever comes first triggers the service. Set your intervals from the manufacturer's recommendations and your own policy, then stick to them.

Running an in-house shop: work orders, technicians, parts

Most cities of any size run their own shop. That means you're managing:

  • Work orders. Every repair and PM should have one. It's the record of what was done, who did it, how long it took, and what parts went on the unit.
  • Technicians. Scheduling, training, certifications and keeping labor hours honest. Tracking time by work order is how you learn what a unit really costs.
  • Parts. A parts room that's too thin keeps units down. One that's too fat ties up budget in stock that sits on a shelf. Knowing what you use, and on which units, is how you find the balance.

In my experience, commercial guides tend to assume you outsource maintenance. Plenty of cities do some of that, but a big share of a city fleet's money goes through the shop, so it deserves real attention.

Fuel management

Fuel is usually one of the biggest line items in any fleet. For a city, the job is controlling who can fuel, tracking which unit got how much, and charging it back to the right department. Fuel cards or a fuel site with unit-level tracking make this much easier. Watch for odd patterns, like a pickup that fuels more than its mileage explains.

Compliance and inspections

What applies depends on your fleet and your state. If you run CDL vehicles, federal DOT rules for drivers and vehicle inspections come into play. Many states add their own inspection or emissions requirements. Emergency vehicles often carry stricter standards than a normal work truck.

I won't list rules here because they vary state to state and change over time. Check with your state agencies and your city attorney. What I will say is this: whatever the rules are, you need a clean record showing when the inspection got done and who did it.

Acquisition and disposal

Buying vehicles means writing specs that fit the job, following procurement rules, and getting the unit upfitted and in service. Disposal is just as important. Holding on to a unit too long costs money in repairs and downtime. Many cities sell surplus units through public auction, and the proceeds may go back toward replacements depending on local policy.

Mixed municipal fleets

In my experience, this is the part commercial guides skip almost entirely. A city fleet is rarely a fleet of one kind of vehicle. A typical list includes:

  • Light-duty: sedans, pickups, SUVs and vans for inspectors, parks and admin.
  • Heavy equipment: loaders, graders, backhoes, sweepers and dump trucks.
  • Emergency vehicles: police units and, in many cities, fire apparatus.
  • Seasonal equipment: plows, spreaders and leaf vacuums.
  • Small engines: mowers, trimmers, chainsaws, pumps and generators.

Each group has its own PM needs. Each group gets used differently. And each group usually belongs to a different department with its own budget.

Small engines often get ignored. They're cheap one at a time, but a parks department can have dozens of them, and they add up. If you don't track them, you can't tell which ones are eating money.

Budgeting, chargebacks and replacement planning

The money side is where a public fleet manager earns trust or loses it.

Cost per unit and lifecycle cost

Start with the basics. For every unit, you should be able to say what it cost to buy, what it has cost to maintain and fuel, and how much downtime it has had. Cost per mile works for road vehicles. Cost per hour works better for equipment.

Lifecycle cost puts that together over the unit's whole life. It's the number that tells you when a unit has crossed the line from "worth fixing" to "time to replace."

Department chargebacks and internal service funds

Many cities run fleet as an internal service. Departments pay the fleet fund for the vehicles they use, through monthly rates, actual repair and fuel charges, or a mix of both. That's a chargeback.

Chargebacks work when departments trust the numbers. They fall apart when a department director gets a bill and can't see what it's for. Give each department a clear view of its own units, its work orders and its costs. That one step heads off a lot of arguments.

Building a replacement plan council will approve

Council doesn't want to hear "the truck is old." They want to see why replacing it now is the better use of money. A good replacement plan:

  • Lists every unit with age, miles or hours, and condition.
  • Shows lifecycle cost and recent repair history.
  • Ranks units by need, not by which department asks loudest.
  • Ties to a capital replacement fund so purchases are planned years ahead.

If you've kept good work order records, most of this builds itself. If you haven't, start now. Next year's budget meeting will go better.

Buying fleet software as a government

Buying software in the public sector is its own process. Depending on the cost and your local rules, you may need formal quotes or a full RFP. Some cities use cooperative purchasing contracts, such as those run through Sourcewell or NASPO ValuePoint (if your state participates), where another agency has already done the competitive bid and you can buy off it if your rules allow. Check with your purchasing office before assuming a vendor is on one.

Things I'd ask any vendor:

  • Can it handle PM by miles, hours and days on the same unit?
  • Can it handle small engines and heavy equipment, not just cars and trucks?
  • Can the city bring its current unit list and history in with a CSV file?
  • How does it handle user roles, so a technician, a department head and an admin each see the right things?
  • Can departments see their own units and costs?
  • Who owns the data, and how does the city get it out if it switches systems?

Ask for a demo using your own units, not a canned sample fleet.

Public records, audits and data retention

In most states, fleet maintenance records are likely public records. That means someone can ask for them, and an auditor can review them. Your state's records laws and your city's retention schedule decide what you keep and for how long. Ask your city clerk or records officer.

The practical takeaway: keep a complete history on every unit. Who did the work, when, what was found, what parts went on, and who signed off. Paper in a filing cabinet makes that hard to find later. A searchable digital record makes it easy, as long as it's backed up and kept for the required period.

Grants and funding for fleet modernization

There are federal and state programs that help public agencies fund cleaner vehicles, alternative fuels and charging. They change often, and eligibility depends on where you are and what you're buying. That's why I'm not listing specific programs here. Check with your state energy office, your regional council of governments, and your city's grants staff. Whatever you apply for, good fleet data makes the application stronger.

Fleet management software (FMIS) vs. telematics: what a city needs

People mix these two up all the time.

Telematics is the hardware and service that tracks where a vehicle is and how it's being driven: GPS location, speed, idling, engine codes. It's useful, especially for routing and safety.

A fleet management information system (FMIS) is the system of record for the fleet itself. Units, PM schedules, work orders, parts, labor, fuel, costs and replacement planning. It's where the shop lives. The FAQ below has a short definition too.

A city that runs its own shop needs an FMIS first. Telematics can feed it mileage and engine hours, which saves typing, but GPS dots on a map won't tell you whether a loader is overdue for service or what it cost the street department last year.

Several established FMIS products serve public fleets. They differ in size, setup effort and cost. Look at a few, and judge them on how well they fit the way your shop actually works.

Getting started: a checklist for small cities

If you're starting from spreadsheets or paper, here's where I'd begin:

  1. Build a complete unit list: every vehicle, piece of equipment and small engine, with the department that owns it.
  2. Record current miles or hours for every unit.
  3. Set PM intervals for each unit type by miles, hours or days, based on manufacturer guidance and your policy.
  4. Write a PM checklist for each unit type so every technician checks the same things.
  5. Open a work order for every repair and PM, with labor and parts recorded.
  6. Track fuel by unit.
  7. Decide who needs access and what each person should see.
  8. Give each department a view of its own units and costs.
  9. Set up chargebacks that departments can follow line by line.
  10. Track a few KPIs every month: availability, PM on-time rate, cost per mile or hour, and technician productivity.
  11. Draft a replacement plan ranked by need and lifecycle cost.
  12. Confirm your records retention schedule with the clerk.
  13. If you're choosing software, make sure your unit list can come in by CSV.

You don't have to do all of it at once. Get the unit list and PM schedule right first. Everything else builds on those two.

FAQ

What does a municipal fleet manager do?

A municipal fleet manager keeps the city's vehicles and equipment safe and ready to work at a cost the city can defend. That covers preventive maintenance, running the shop, fuel, compliance, buying and disposing of units, department chargebacks, and replacement planning for council.

What is an FMIS?

An FMIS is a fleet management information system. It's the system of record for your fleet: units, PM schedules, work orders, parts, labor, fuel and costs. It's different from telematics, which tracks location and driving.

How is government fleet management different from commercial?

Public fleets focus on service to residents instead of profit. They follow public procurement rules, budget on a fiscal year, answer to council, keep records that may be public, and usually run a mixed fleet with an in-house shop.

How do cities buy fleet software without a full RFP?

Depending on local rules, some cities use cooperative purchasing contracts, where another agency already ran the competitive bid. Smaller purchases may only need quotes. Your purchasing office decides what applies.

How often should city vehicles get preventive maintenance?

Follow the manufacturer's recommendations and your own policy for each unit type. Track intervals by miles, engine hours or calendar days, and service the unit at whichever comes first.

What is a fleet chargeback?

A chargeback is how the fleet fund bills other departments for the vehicles and services they use. It can be a monthly rate, actual repair and fuel costs, or a mix. Clear, unit-level detail helps departments trust the bill.

How do I justify vehicle replacement to council?

Show the numbers. Present each unit's age, miles or hours, condition, repair history and lifecycle cost, rank units by need, and tie purchases to a planned capital replacement fund.

Do small engines and equipment belong in fleet software?

Yes. Mowers, saws, pumps and generators need service too, and together they can cost a department real money. Tracking them by hours or days shows which ones are worth keeping.

Are fleet maintenance records public records?

In most states, they likely are. Your state's records laws and your city's retention schedule decide what you keep, for how long, and what can be released. Check with your city clerk or attorney.

What KPIs should a public fleet track?

Start with fleet availability, PM on-time rate, cost per mile or per hour, and technician productivity. Together they show whether your units are ready and what it costs to keep them that way.
